## The Brief

Downlow's weekday lunch daypart was sitting well below its evening and weekend trade, even though
the brand's cheeky "secret sauce" positioning is exactly the kind of personality that wins a fast
lunch decision. The brief: design a full paid-media campaign — not just ad copy — that could win
two very different lunch audiences near Downlow's CBD and university-adjacent stores using a single
anchor offer, without asking the business to build new ordering infrastructure or shift its brand
voice to feel like a generic discount promo.

<div class="cs-accordion not-prose">

<details open>
<summary>Methodology</summary>
<div class="cs-panel">

- **Audience-first diagnosis**: separated the lunch market into two genuinely different buyers —
  office workers (efficiency-led, decide 15–30 minutes out) and students (value-led, decide
  last-minute off peer influence) — rather than writing one generic "lunch deal" persona, since
  each group responds to a different trigger even though the $15 offer stays identical
- **Channel role separation**: assigned Google Search the job of capturing the exact moment
  someone is already deciding where to eat, and Meta the job of building the "what's for lunch"
  consideration set before that search happens — so the two platforms complement rather than
  duplicate each other
- **Hyperlocal, dayparted targeting**: built location targeting around actual store trade areas
  (Commerce Street, Grafton, Courtenay Place, Willis Lane, The Base) and restricted delivery to
  weekdays, 10:30am–2pm, so spend only runs when and where a lunch decision is actually happening
- **Assumption-labelled planning**: every budget, bidding, and benchmark figure was sourced from
  New Zealand QSR industry data and explicitly flagged as an assumption rather than presented as
  a result, since no campaign had run yet — a discipline that keeps the blueprint honest and
  immediately usable once real spend and margin data are available

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>What was delivered</summary>
<div class="cs-panel">

- A complete Google Ads architecture — Search campaign structure across three ad groups
  (local intent, value intent, branded/defensive), a full keyword strategy with negatives,
  budget and bidding plan, dayparted ad schedule, and all extensions (location, call, sitelinks,
  callouts, structured snippets)
- Full Google ad copy — 15 headlines, 4 descriptions, callouts and sitelinks, each written to
  answer the two objections that stall a lunch click: "is it fast?" and "what do I actually get?"
- A three-campaign Meta Ads structure (Awareness, Prospecting, Retargeting) with interest,
  custom, and lookalike audience definitions, a day-by-day retargeting sequence, and two parallel
  creative tracks — an "Office Worker" cut and a "Student" cut — built for the same offer
- A full creative direction guide (photography style, colour palette, typography, brand-voice
  consistency) and a KPI benchmark dashboard with a phased four-week optimisation plan, so the
  blueprint reads as an operating manual rather than a one-off recommendation

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>The result</summary>
<div class="cs-panel">

- **A launch-ready campaign package**, not a concept deck — every element (budget split, ad
  schedule, targeting radius, copy, creative brief, KPI targets) is specific enough to hand
  straight to a media buyer for a real four-week weekday test (~NZ$1,200 total Google spend)
- **Targeting logic anchored to real trade areas** — store-by-store radius targeting and
  campus-aware keyword themes, rather than broad city-wide guessing that would burn budget on
  low-intent clicks
- **A structure built to scale, not just launch** — the four-week optimisation plan and the
  "validated playbook" framing were designed from day one to turn a single successful test into a
  rollout plan for the rest of Downlow's network, once real performance data exists to act on

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>Reflection</summary>
<div class="cs-panel">

The hardest part of this blueprint wasn't the media plan — it was resisting the pull to write two
separate campaigns for two separate audiences. Office workers and students want genuinely
different things from the same $15, and the plan only works because the offer stays fixed while
the message, creative, and even the ad schedule flex around each group's real decision pattern.
The other discipline worth carrying into every planning document like this is labelling
assumptions as assumptions: a blueprint that's honest about what it doesn't know yet (actual
margin, actual current spend) is far more useful to a real business than one that quietly implies
results it hasn't earned.
