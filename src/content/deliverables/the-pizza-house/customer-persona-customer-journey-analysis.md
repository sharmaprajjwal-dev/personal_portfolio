Personas are evidence-informed decision archetypes rather than real named customers. Names,
quotes, and personal details are synthetic; needs and journey findings are grounded in the
business proposition, public reviews, market research, and observable digital behaviour.
</p>

## The Brief

The label “pizza customer” was too broad to guide meaningful decisions. The restaurant serves
different occasions, and the person organising each occasion carries different risks: choosing
somewhere a peer group accepts, finding a setting suitable for a date, coordinating a family with
mixed preferences, or completing a convenient repeat order with minimal effort.

The brief was to identify those decision patterns, show where information and trust mattered,
and map the full journey from awareness through consideration, action, experience, repeat
purchase, and advocacy.

<div class="cs-accordion not-prose">

<details open>
<summary>Methodology</summary>
<div class="cs-panel">

- **Evidence synthesis**: combined the restaurant's offer and location with public reviews,
  competitor evidence, demographic context, and local restaurant-choice research
- **Occasion-based segmentation**: grouped customers by the job they were trying to complete,
  decision authority, urgency, and perceived social or practical risk
- **Persona development**: created four executive archetypes covering peer groups,
  experience-sensitive social dining, family coordination, and convenience-led repeat ordering
- **Journey mapping**: assessed discovery, comparison, validation, action, fulfilment,
  post-purchase evaluation, repeat behaviour, and advocacy
- **Friction and trust analysis**: linked information gaps, menu clarity, reviews, atmosphere,
  price, location, response speed, and service consistency to customer decisions

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>What was delivered</summary>
<div class="cs-panel">

- A customer overview and segmentation model based on occasions rather than broad demographics
- Four detailed executive personas with goals, triggers, barriers, proof requirements, digital
  behaviours, preferred actions, and business implications
- A needs analysis distinguishing functional, emotional, social, and confidence requirements
- A complete journey map connecting customer questions and friction to relevant channels and
  operating moments
- Analysis of digital discovery, comparison behaviour, trust and purchase drivers, pain points,
  loyalty considerations, and lifetime-value questions requiring future data
- Prioritised customer insights for website, local search, content, conversion, reputation, and
  service design

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>The result</summary>
<div class="cs-panel">

- **Leadership gained a more useful definition of the customer**: the organiser and the occasion
  became more important than a generic interest in pizza
- **Decision-support priorities became concrete** — menu breadth, price clarity, proof, location,
  atmosphere, fulfilment, and the next action all reduce different forms of customer risk
- **The group organiser emerged as a critical conversion audience** because that person must
  satisfy multiple preferences and carries the social cost of a poor choice
- **Later channel strategies became customer-led**, with content, pages, calls to action, and
  review proof mapped to specific journey stages rather than published in isolation

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>Reflection</summary>
<div class="cs-panel">

The analysis showed why demographic labels alone are weak tools for restaurant strategy. Two
customers of the same age can need entirely different information when one is organising a family
meal and the other wants a quick repeat order.

The strongest customer insight was that confidence is part of conversion. Clear menus, credible
reviews, current practical information, and predictable service do more than answer questions;
they reduce the risk carried by the person making the choice for everyone else.

</div>
</details>
